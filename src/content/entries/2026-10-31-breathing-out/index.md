Wa is the slow mode, deliberately airy, but keeping it devoid of any background music made it feel comparatively unfinished, once Hap had its beat.

So I started with a beat for Wa too, deliberately slower paced and spacious, of course. I recorded my own voice making a deep "doom" sound while gently tapping a pad, thinking of a janggu player carrying a pansori singer, believing it to be a great idea. In practice it fell apart. The freeform puzzle solving of Wa just doesn't sit on a regular beat.

The idea that eventually worked was a pad: a soft, low chord held under your notes. It doesn't follow a clock, and it never nudges you. Your clears progress the chord, patiently following your pace, whatever that is.

This design must survive every kind of player. Someone who clears a board in a minute. Someone who takes five or ten. And someone like me, who leaves a board open on a second screen and comes back now and then. A monotonous tone that droned on for twenty minutes would wear on anyone.

So each chord gets one long breath. It holds for nine seconds, then breathes out, and about 36 seconds after it arrived it is gone. A board left alone falls quiet. When you come back for your next clear, the chord wakes up again, a little softer. And once 60 percent of the board is cleared, a faint high layer joins, so the last stretch opens up.

While I preferred it when a chord breathed out over a full minute, it turned out to take a toll on performance: in the browser one sound that long would have taken about 23 MB of memory. The compromise was to make the effect from short breaths: a six-second swell, started every three seconds, each one a little quieter than the last. Now the sustain takes about 5 MB. It is shorter, but it still feels patient.

An honest hallmark of good sound design is whether players keep the sound on while they play. I will have no way of knowing what they do, but I hope Klaar!'s audio design enriches their enjoyment.
